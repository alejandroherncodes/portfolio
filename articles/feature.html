NOW RAVING: Hollywood, Infected

Tastemakers Magazine
Alejandro Hernandez · April 22, 2026

A film score has one job: telling you how to feel before you've figured it out yourself. But at some point, deep rolling basslines started edging out the strings. Not that it's something to complain about. Who's really asking for the orchestra back, anyway? Even so, electronic scoring is nothing new. Synthesizers have been creeping into the cinema since the seventies, but for a long time they’ve largely stayed in one lane: horror, sci-fi, the margins. Techno is a different question. What changed and made directors want to try to bring the warehouse into the theater?

Gaspar Noé was among the first to treat film music as a drug delivery mechanism. His 2009 psychedelic melodrama Enter the Void follows a drug dealer's consciousness drifting through Tokyo after he's killed. Noé originally wanted Daft Punk's Thomas Bangalter to score the film, but Bangalter was already committed to scoring Tron: Legacy. Instead, he handed Noé a palette of drones and ambient sounds. Noé curated around that palette using experimental and electronic music of the sixties and seventies, creating a collage of electronic smog and ambient abstraction that dissolves the screen and drops you into the protagonist's disembodied spirit. Mainstream critics didn't quite have the framework for a soundtrack like this, but the film found its audience the way midnight movies do: late at night, among people who needed a two-and-a-half-hour ego death.

The following year, Daft Punk’s score for Tron: Legacy made the case for electronic scoring as a blockbuster language to a much larger room. The biggest electronic act on the planet worked on a film carved to their music, giving Tron’s digital universe a pulse and identity it didn’t have before. Wendy Carlos had used her synthesizer to imitate an orchestra for the original 1982 film, but Daft Punk brought in 85 live musicians and drowned them in an electronic mix so completely that they sounded synthetic anyway. Turns out, $400 million at the box office had quite a way of fighting the argument against electronic music's place in Hollywood blockbusters.

Shortly after, Nine Inch Nails members Trent Reznor and Atticus Ross composed their first score for The Social Network, and the Academy handed them an Oscar for it. Droning synth pads and processed keys accumulate so slowly you don’t register the betrayals behind the founding of Facebook, the score composed to be the interface where the film’s lawsuits and fallouts arrive pre-accepted and scrolled past. They went on to confirm their magic wasn’t a one-off with their work on The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo (2011) and Gone Girl (2014). Each of those movies, all directed by David Fincher, touches on the performance of a normal life while something corrodes underneath, and in every case, the score is the corrosion.

Meanwhile, Noé was busy plotting something much more deranged. His 2018 feature, Climax, runs on cold club classic needle-drops and a new song by Daft Punk. The film follows a French dance troupe in an empty school, celebrating the end of their final rehearsal before the night spirals into paranoia and violence after someone secretly spikes the sangria with LSD. Before it kicks in, Noé delivers a 5-minute dance sequence, filmed in one shot, set to Cerrone's “Supernature.” It’s the only part of the film you'll ever want to rewatch, if you can stomach knowing what comes after. What follows is a not-so-gradual descent into hell that the music mockingly ignores. Aphex Twin and Dopplereffekt continue at full volume in complete indifference to the atrocities taking place on the screen. That’s, in some ways, one of the most disturbing things about the movie: the soundtrack is a chemical pulse baked into the concrete walls, never intended to stop even as the party cannibalizes itself. This ninety-minute bad trip will implicate you with the rest of the room and leave you crawling away from the scene, terrified of ever touching a psychedelic drug.

That same heartless pulse soon became more intentional after seeping into indie black comedy and prestige horror. Gen Z group-hysteria satire, Bodies Bodies Bodies (2022) was driven by maximalist dance tracks that belong to the same social world that causes the damage. By 2024, Hollywood was commissioning original scores that adapted Noe's sensory hostility. Coralie Fargeat's The Substance won Cannes’ top prize, and its signature motif is a synthesized whirl each time the titular substance is injected, birthing a clone or forcing the switch back. It hits like a luxury drug, the body's excitement indistinguishable from its alarm. Fargeat handed the score to London producer Raffertie and asked for "electronic violence and roughness." He constructed something that mirrored the film's own body-horror logic the way he builds his experimental club music: kicks and sub-bass engineered for speakers that are able to rearrange your internal organs.

Earlier that year, Reznor and Ross scored Challengers, a non-linear love triangle with a Berlin-rave heartbeat in place of traditional romance. Hypnotic synth pulses and four-on-the-floor kicks build without release, pushing the tension right up to the edge before resetting. The film surrenders its pacing to the score, relentless electronic loops reorganizing the central dramatic arc. The arpeggiated synths and industrial grooves never resolve so much as reset, every volley returning desire and rivalry to the same locked cycle. Reznor and Ross sustained that friction by releasing a continuous 28-minute version of the score, mixed by Boys Noize, to let the tension exist on its own as a techno record.

In 2025, Sirât opened with seventeen straight minutes of bass-heavy primal techno, a massive endurance challenge for the speakers and everyone sitting in front of them. A father and son comb through the raves of Morocco in search of their missing daughter, and Kangding Ray's score drops you right into the clouds of dust. It then dematerializes slowly into ethereal soundscapes as the desert absorbs the ravers whole, the music hardening into a landscape no longer tied to the bodies that once moved with it.

That brings us to The Moment (2026), a mockumentary about pop stardom. A.G. Cook's score keeps Charli XCX's voice entirely separate, letting it exist only on screen while the music runs independently as the industry's operating system. Squelching acid lines and throbbing basslines drop without warning into empty space, the chords neither here nor there. The music doesn't resolve so much as vanish, dropping the scene into silence before the next loop intrudes. All of this is layered over visuals strobing so fast they could send someone to the hospital. By the end, her voice enters only as distortion, biological residue digested by the industrial machinery that once rejected her.

Call it the club-ification of narrative. Nothing else quite captures the present as accurately as rhythm-first scoring, and directors are noticing. The sound of the warehouse has become a precise language not only for desire, dread, and transformation, but for systems that act on the body and keep running after it’s gone. On the dance floor, the music is a service: a collaborative loop designed to keep the dancer moving. Inside the theater, that service is severed: the loop keeps running, and it's no longer for you.
