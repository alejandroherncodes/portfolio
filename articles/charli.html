Charli Goes Gothic.

Tastemakers Magazine
Alejandro Hernandez · March 14, 2026 8.0 / 10

Moldy | Stale | Edible | Fresh | Tasty!
Tasty Tracks: Dying for You, Seeing Things, My Reminder, Funny Mouth

Wuthering Heights was born out of exhaustion and the strange clarity that follows it. By her own account, Charli xcx reached burn-out long before the Brat-mania was beginning to wear off of the rest of us. She wanted a reset, but what she really needed was an out. When Emerald Fennell suggested Charli make a song or two for a new movie adaptation of the 1847 gothic classic, Charli counteroffered with a whole album and set out to make something "elegant and brutal."

Wuthering Heights is Charli's second soundtrack album and her seventh studio record. Of course, this isn't the first time a pop artist has translated the literary classic into the contemporary. Kate Bush did it in 1978, her otherworldly debut single still lingering in Charli’s work. Charli does the same, and makes it entirely hers. Working inside someone else's world is not only central to the way this album breathes, but the reason it buckles at certain points.

Yet, the true touchstone for this album is Charli’s 2013 debut, True Romance, the desperately bright and emotionally uninhibited record she spent the following decade running away from. She turns back toward it with her ear tuned to Nine Inch Nails and The Velvet Underground: strings, gothic claustrophobia, and the immensity of a woman alone inside feelings all too large for her. Unmistakably Charli, Wuthering Heights presents untamable emotions boiling underneath heavy layers of processing.

Strings thread through and between the album’s more electronic moments, sometimes in harmony and sometimes in dissonance. The album opens with “House,” a spoken word track where The Velvet Underground’s John Cale murmurs a warning over atonal strings: "If there's beauty, say it's enough." The moment lands slowly, like a last breath before hypothermia sets in. It’s not the cozy type of cold, but the cold of the Yorkshire moors burrowing deep beneath your skin. Emily Brontë understood how to capture that. Charli xcx seems to have felt it too.

The imagery of houses, altars, and chains scribble love architecturally into a labyrinth of desire you can't escape. “Wall of Sound" discovers an ache buried alive, lyrics reaching across a distance they're not able to close. Suffocating strings wrap around the deliberately restrained vocals until raw, intimate distance is all you feel: the very definition of yearning. Unable to break away, "Chains of Love," pits cinematic strings against crisp percussion while the melody loops back on itself until you feel the chains before she names them. Then, Charli takes the chains and builds a shrine. "Altars" turns obsession into blind worship with eyes wide open, the lyrics throwing themselves down at the feet of something that would never break the fall.

The middle stretch of the album is where mud starts to track onto the carpet. "Eyes of the World" should be one of the record’s defining moments. Sky Ferreira sounds like someone recording from captivity, which suits the album’s themes, but the orchestration repeatedly drifts without giving her anything to collapse onto. That fault moves in the opposite direction on "Out of Myself," with the song’s composition doing the heavy lifting as Charli snarls masochistic pleas that seem to bleed in place. The album's stickiest hook doesn't bother with any of this showmanship: "I think I'm seein' shadows of your hair / I think I'm seein' things." In spite of its plain production and simple writing, "Seeing Things" lands harder than some of the record's more grand gestures.

Still, the rot in the walls only amplifies the sublime. Elegance and brutality collide head-on in “Dying for You,” production reaching for a dance-rock track while inconsolable lyrics hemorrhage underneath. It’s the record’s clearest gem and the friction between pleasure and pain evokes exactly what Emily Brontë set out to capture. "Funny Mouth" holds the wreckage between its teeth: strings swell and writhe against brittle synths catching static in the bass churn. The vocal, stretching so taut it warps, takes the contortion in stride. Everything empties out slowly until the album wearily surrenders with a bitter smile.

Wuthering Heights stands out not because it's Charli’s strongest album, but because it demonstrates that she can work entirely inside someone else's world and still sound like no one other than herself. Wuthering Heights is not the type of record that will ever be the public's favorite, but it’s the type to reward the “angels” over the “brats.”
